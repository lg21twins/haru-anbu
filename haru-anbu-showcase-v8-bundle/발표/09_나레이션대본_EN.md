## n02 · Overview

> In every care setting, there are always three people.
> The elder receiving care, the care team beside them, and the family worrying from afar.
> The trouble is, these three see the same day from separate places, each on their own.
> Haru Anbu connects those three perspectives through five screens — one for each role.
> An elder's daily record passes through the care team's review and reaches the family automatically.
> From an emergency call to medication checks, daily news, and visit notes — record it once, and it reaches exactly the person who needs it.

## n03 · Problem ① · the heaviest question

> For a child living far from home, a parent's wellbeing is the heaviest question of every morning.
> You tell yourself they're probably fine — but a quiet "probably?" always follows.
> Even a phone call, hearing their voice, doesn't tell you the whole day.
> Family, care team, elder — they live the same day, yet it scatters into different languages.
> One phone call can't truly set anyone's mind at ease.

## n04 · Problem ② · why now

> This is Korea's aging curve.
> To go from an aging society to a super-aged one, France took a hundred and fifty-four years, and the United States, eighty-eight.
> Korea took just twenty-four.
> In that time, households where someone over sixty-five lives alone reached two point one million, and elderly poverty became the highest in the OECD.
> The number of people who need care is rising this fast — yet the way families and clinicians stay connected is still a single phone call.
> This is where technology hasn't kept pace. And this is exactly the problem we set out to solve.

## n08 · Design ① · brand mark

> That promise lives in the design itself.
> Look at the two curves in our logo — one is the family far away, the other the patient close by.
> The care team's hand between them brings the two together at a single point.

## n09 · Design ② · typography

> We chose the typefaces with the care floor in mind.
> The base is Pretendard — its letters and numbers stay crisp, easy on the eyes even at large sizes.
> And when Chinese appears, it flows naturally into Noto Sans SC.
> Quite a few caregivers come from abroad, after all.
> So that any language reads comfortably, we kept multilingual legibility in mind from the very start.

## n10 · Design ③ · color system

> We used color as a language to tell the three apart.
> Family is blue — a tone of trust and reassurance, a promise to loved ones far away.
> The care team is green — the clinical color of done, normal, and safe.
> The patient is orange — warm but never heavy, suited to large text and wide touch targets.
> So even when one screen holds many kinds of information, a single glance at the color tells you whose it is.

## n11 · Design ④ · icons

> Every icon belongs to one family.
> A single set of solid Fluent icons — medication, an S O S, a meal — all share the same feel.
> If the drawing style changed from screen to screen, an elder would have to learn it all over again, every time.
> So once it's learned, it reads the same in any app. We held that consistency.

## n12 · Design ⑤ · UI kit

> And these aren't decorative mockups.
> The medication progress, the mood score, the sleep graph, the AI chat you're seeing — these are the real components, used as they are in the apps.
> We built all five apps from the same parts, so whichever screen you open, the familiar feel carries through.
> We made the design the product, and the product the design.

## n13 · Persona · one day, three points of view

> The same day looks like this — different, depending on who's looking.
> For the family — even far away, news arrives every day. One short daily report, and you know right away they're well.
> For the elder — one button is enough. Large text, easy touch; sending word takes just once.
> For the care team — the moment they record it, that effort reaches the family as it is.
> A single entry reaches all three people, each in its own form.

## n14 · Service · six core capabilities

> Even with five apps, six capabilities run across them all.
> One — the AI peace-of-mind score. It distills meals, medication, sleep, and activity into a number from zero to a hundred, so family can read yesterday in five seconds.
> Two — the automatic daily report. Each morning, the day's records turn into a family card and send themselves.
> Three — the three-minute dawn S O S. From the call to alerting caregiver, clinician, and family, it connects in about three minutes on average.
> Four — anomaly detection. I catch signals that differ from the usual, early, and warn the care team before anything happens.
> Five — unified handover across the three perspectives. One record reaches all three people, each in their own language.
> Six — voice handover summaries. I take a caregiver's spoken handover and pull out just what matters, automatically.
> Whichever screen you open, these six are always at work together.
